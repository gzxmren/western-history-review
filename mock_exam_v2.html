Source A — Primary Source

The following is an excerpt from a pamphlet written by Abbé Sieyès in 1789, just before the French Revolution:

"What is the Third Estate? Everything. What has it been in the political order until now? Nothing. What does it ask for? To become something. … The Third Estate bears all the burdens of the state. The nobles enjoy privileges without contributing anything. The Church owns vast lands and collects tithes but pays no taxes. Is it not time to change this unjust system?"

— Adapted from Abbé Sieyès, What is the Third Estate? (1789)

S-T-A-R prompt: S = Sieyès, a clergyman who supported the Third Estate, writing on the eve of the Revolution. T = angry, provocative, rhetorical ("Is it not time…?"). A = the Three Estates system is deeply unjust. R = Sieyès has a clear political agenda — to inspire change — so he may emphasise inequality and minimise other factors.

Source B — Primary Source

The following is a description of factory conditions during the Industrial Revolution, written by a British government inspector in 1842:

"Children as young as six or seven years old work in the coal mines and cotton factories. They work fourteen to sixteen hours a day, six days a week. The air is filled with coal dust and cotton fibres. The machines are dangerous, and accidents are common. Workers who are injured are often dismissed without pay. Many families live in crowded, unsanitary rooms near the factories."

— Adapted from reports of the Children's Employment Commission (1842)

S-T-A-R prompt: S = British government inspector (official report). T = factual and descriptive but with a sympathetic tone toward workers. A = working conditions were harsh and dangerous, especially for children. R = government commission was tasked with investigating conditions; its purpose was to gather evidence for reform, so it may highlight the worst cases to build a case for new laws.

Source C — Primary Source

The following is a statement by Enlightenment thinker John Locke:

"All men are born with certain natural rights — the rights to life, liberty, and property. The purpose of government is to protect these rights. If a government fails to do so, or if it violates these rights, the people have the right to overthrow it and establish a new government."

— Based on John Locke, Two Treatises of Government (1689)

S-T-A-R prompt: S = John Locke, English philosopher writing after the Glorious Revolution (1688). T = philosophical and principled — this is a reasoned argument, not emotional. A = government's legitimacy comes from protecting natural rights; if it fails, people may replace it. R = influential text whose ideas were adopted by revolutionaries, but Locke's focus on "property" reflects his time (wealthy landowner's perspective).

Source D — Primary Source

The following is a description of life in a factory town, written by a French visitor to Manchester in 1835:

"Manchester is a city of contrasts. On one side, I see towering factories, smoking chimneys, and the constant hum of machinery — symbols of progress. On the other, I walk through filthy streets where ragged children play in the mud and thin, pale-faced workers pour out of the factory gates like ghosts. This is the price of industrial progress."

— Adapted from Alexis de Tocqueville, Journeys to England and Ireland (1835)
