The concept of the Renaissance

(a) What was the Renaissance? _ Refer to 2A Attached Notes-01.

The Renaissance was a cultural and intellectual movement
that took place in Europe between the 14th and 17th centuries
(late Medieval Times). It first started in Italy and later spread

throughout Europe.

The word ‘Renaissance’ means ‘revival’ or ‘rebirth’. The

Renaissance refers to the revival of the ancient Greco-Roman
civilization. During this period, Europeans innovated and
carried forward the classical civilization. The Renaissance
laid the foundations for development of modern European
civilization and was the transitional period from Medieval

Times to Modern Times.

From God-centred to human centred: the Renaissance and the Reformation

(ii) Emphasizing humanism

Europeans were no longer God-centred but were more concerned
with worldly affairs. They regarded humans as the centre of the
universe. Humans should have independent thinking and develop

their capacities. This human-centred idea was known as ‘humanism’.
(iii) Challenging tradition

Influenced by humanism, Europeans stressed the spirit of
inquiry and rational thinking. They challenged traditional
ideas and the Church teachings, and proposed new ideas. Many

important scientific discoveries and inventions were made.

Production of books during the early Medieval Times

Before the invention of the movable-type printing press in Europe, books
were copied out by priests. This helped preserve European civilization but
it took a long time to copy a book. Therefore, books were expensive and
ordinary people could not afford them.

Invention and contribution of the movable-type printing press

In 1450, Gutenberg made a mechanical movable-
type printing press to print the Bible. Gutenberg's printing
technology spread quickly in Europe. From 1450 to 1500,
about ten million books were printed. With the invention
of the movable-type printing press, books could be printed
faster and produced at a lower cost. More people could
buy books. This helped the spread of ideas in Europe
during the Renaissance.

The Age of Reason: the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment

i?) The Scientific Revolution

Scientific study became popular in 16th-century Europe, but it mainly
aimed to revive the ancient Greco-Roman scientific knowledge. In the
17th century, the ‘Scientific Revolution’ started. Many new scientific

theories were proposed, marking a leap forward in the technological

development of modern Europe.

The first revolution in Western history?

The English word ‘revolution’ comes from the French word 'revolucién',
meaning ‘periodic cycle . According to Harvard professor Shapin, the word
acquired another meaning of ‘unprecedented’ by the 18th century. It was
used by French scholars to describe the great changes in science in 17th-
century Europe. This showed that the first revolution in Western history
started in science (the Scientific Revolution), but the word is commonly
adopted to describe political events later on.

(a) Causes of the Scientific Revolution

(i) Encouragement of the Renaissance and the printing press

Renaissance scholars carried out experiments, and proposed scientific
theories which contradicted the teachings of the Church. Modern Western
science began to rise (see p.21). The development of the printing press
increased book circulation. This promoted academic exchanges and

created favourable conditions for the start of the Scientific Revolution.
(ii) Encouragement of the Voyages of Discovery

Between the 15th and 16th centuries, European navigators started a
series of naval explorations, known as the 'Voyages of Discovery’. They
opened up new sea routes and discovered unknown lands (such as the

Americas). Some even completed global circumnavigation.

Technological advancement: the Industrial Revolution

八 Causes of the Industrial Revolution
(a) Rise in population

In the 18th and 19th centuries, the population of Europe grew
rapidly. This provided labour for the development of industry
and trade. It also led to a rising demand for goods, and thus

encouraged mass industrial production.

(b) Development of world trade

From the 15th century onward, world trade centred on

Europe grew rapidly. In order to meet the global demand for
manufactured goods, Europeans introduced new machines and

technologies in production.

(c) Accumulation of capital

European merchants accumulated a huge amount of capital
from foreign trade. They then largely invested on new machines

as well as technological innovations to increase production and

earn higher profits.

(d) Development and application of science

After the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution, Europe
had greatly advanced in science. With the support of merchants,

European scientists sought to apply their knowledge to improve

industrial technologies.

Topic 5 The rise of modern Europe

Supplementary notes (p.6)

The concept of the Renaissance

* The word 'Renaissance' comes from French. It was first used by an
Italian artist named Vasari (ALi). In 1550, he stated in his book,
Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects ( (BR
傑出 的 畫 家 、 雕 塑 家 、 建 築 家 傳記 》), that art was 'born' in ancient
Egypt and prospered in ancient Greece and Rome; it died because of
the decline of the Roman Empire and the suppression of the Church.

* Vasari used the Italian phrase sua rinascita’ to describe the revival of
art’ in the 13th century. In the 19th century, French scholars translated
this Italian word into French as ‘Renaissance’.

Supplementary notes (p.6)
Medieval Times

* After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in AD 476, the old Roman
lands in Europe were divided among rival peoples. In the 14th century
AD, the Renaissance started in Italy. Scholars at the time called the
earlier period (1.e. Medieval Times) the Dark Ages. They believed that
it was a period of lawlessness and disorder. They also believed that the
development of Europe during the period lagged behind ancient Greece
and Rome, and might even have regressed.

* The Renaissance aimed to revive the ancient Greco-Roman civilization.

Supplementary notes (p.7)
Serf

During Medieval Times, peasants were divided into freemen and serfs.
Freemen only needed to pay taxes to their lords, while serfs were the
property of their lords. Serfs had to serve and pay taxes to their lords for
life. They could not leave the manors without the permission of their lords.

Supplementary notes (p.8)
The Crusades

* The Crusades were military campaigns started by European
Christians against Muslims in the Middle East. In 1095, the Pope
called upon Christians to join a holy war against Muslims to
recapture Jerusalem, their Holy Land. This started the Crusades.

* Jerusalem is a holy city for Jews, Christians and Muslims. In AD
638, Muslims conquered Jerusalem. However, Christians were still
allowed to make pilgrimages to Jerusalem.

* In 1009, Muslims destroyed the Church of the Holy Sepulcher (2
教堂 ). Muslim rulers also started to restrict Christian pilgrimages in
Jerusalem. Relations between Christians and Muslims worsened.
The struggle for the control of Jerusalem became one of the causes
of the Crusades.

* In 1291, the Crusaders' strongholds were captured by the Muslims.
This marked the failure and end of the Crusades.

Supplementary notes (p.8)

Florence

* Florence was an important cultural centre of the Renaissance.
It was founded by the Etruscans in about 200 BC. The present city
of Florence was founded by Julius Caesar in 59 BC. It was used as
a settlement for his veteran soldiers. Florence gradually developed
into a commercial centre.

‧ Inthe 15th century, Florence was ruled by the Medici family.
Florence became an economic and cultural centre on the Italian
peninsula with the support of the Medici family.

Supplementary notes (p.10)

Support of rich merchants

The Medici family members in Florence were mainly intellectual

elites and humanists. After years of education and influence of art,

they valued scholars and artists. Fabulous artwork could also show

a family's refinement, reputation and wealth. Here are some

examples of the support of the Medici family to writers and artists.

‧ Cosimo Medici (#5} 74 52-15 38 Z) sponsored scholar Marsilio
Ficino ( 費 奇 諾 ) to build the Academy, and to translate the works
of Plato and other ancient Greek scholars.

‧ Piero Medici (7138-35443) sponsored artist Sandro Botticelli
( 波 提 切 利 ).

‧ Lorenzo Medici ( 洛 倫 佐 . 瑪 迪 奇 ) sponsored the famous artists
Michelangelo and Raphael.

Historical profile (p.11)

Dante

Dante was born into a noble family in Florence. He studied
philosophy and poetry when he was young. In 1302, Dante
was exiled from Florence after a power struggle. He lived in
different Italian cities and worked for many rulers. He wrote
the Divine Comedy around 1310-1314.

Historical profile (p.11)

Boccaccio

* Boccaccio was born into a merchant family in Florence.

He was a famous Italian writer. His father had sent him to
Naples to study commerce, but he chose to be a writer.
The experience in Naples, however, helped Boccaccio to
compose his great work Decameron later on.

* In 1353, Boccaccio finished the Decameron. The book 1s about a
group of ten young people (seven young women and three young
men). They fled from Florence to the countryside during the
Black Death. They lived in a deserted villa for ten days.

Besides singing and dancing, each person told a story every
day. They finally told 100 stories in total. The stories were
about the reality of life, merchants' intelligence, praise of love,
and the corruption of the royals and the Church.

Historical profile (p.11)
Shakespeare

Shakespeare was born in Stratford-upon-Avon ($25971178).
He began his career in London and became a well-known
playwright. He wrote many famous plays. Today, Shakespeare
is still a very famous playwright.

Supplementary notes (p.13)
The Mona Lisa

The Mona Lisa is a famous painting by Leonardo da Vinci. It is now
kept at the Louvre Museum in France. For years people have been
trying to find out who Mona Lisa was. Her true identity remains
unknown. Some people believe that she was the wife of a wealthy
merchant, Francesco del Giocondo. Some believe that she was the
mistress of a Medici.

Historical profile (p.14)

Leonardo da Vinci

* Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519) was regarded as a 'Renaissance
Man’. He became an apprentice of a famous artist in Florence
when he was young. Six years later, he joined the guild for
painters and became qualified.

* In 1482, he was employed by the Duke of Milan. During this
employment, he painted the Last Supper. In 1516, he accepted
an invitation from the King of France to work and settle there.
He spent the rest of his life in France.

Historical profile (p.14)

Michelangelo

* Michelangelo (1475-1564) learned painting and sculpting from
a famous artist of Florence In his early years. He then worked in
Bologna and Rome. From 1508 to 1512, Michelangelo painted
the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel. Then he returned to Florence to
build churches and libraries.

* From 1534 to 1541, he stayed in Rome again and worked for
the Pope. From 1540 to 1550, he was the architect of St Peter's
Basilica and designed the great dome.

Historical profile (p.14)
Raphael

* Raphael (1483-1520) became famous when he was young.
He was commissioned by nobles and merchants to paint portraits
for them. He studied the painting techniques of Leonardo da Vinc1
to develop his own style.

* In 1508, Raphael was summoned to Rome to paint for the Pope.
In 1511, he completed the School of Athens and became the chief
coordinator of art for the Church.

supplementary notes (p.20)

Gothic architecture

Gothic architecture started in France in the 12th century and
spread throughout Western Europe through the 15th century.
Many European cathedrals were built in this style. Tall pointed
towers were commonly found in Gothic architecture as this style
stressed open space, light and height. Christians used to believe
that taller buildings brought them closer to God.

Supplementary notes (p.20)

St. Peter's Basilica

St. Peter's Basilica is located in the Vatican, the headquarters

of the Catholic Roman Church. The Basilica was built mainly
between 1506 and 1626. Many famous architects, such as
Raphael and Michelangelo, took part in the construction

works. It 1s regarded as one of the most important churches by
Christians as lt was believed to be built over the grave of St.
Peter. Here, many European emperors were crowned in the past.

Supplementary notes (p.21)

Geocentrism

* Before the Renaissance, knowledge about astronomy in Europe
mainly came from ancient Greek astronomer Ptolemy's (#68) #)
book written in 140 BC. The book outlines Aristotle's views on
the structure and origins of the universe, including geocentrism:
the long-standing belief that the Earth was at the centre of the

solar system.

* The Church's ideas about the universe were based on the
explanations of Aristotle and Ptolemy. They were also mixed
with religion: a flat-domed Earth was at the centre; Heaven
was outside the Earth while Hell was below the Earth.

Historical profile (p.21)

Fracastoro

* Fracastoro (1478-1553) was a professor at the University of
Padua (182 5, X=) in Italy. He studied epidemic diseases and
stated that they were spread by tiny particles. Diseases could be
transmitted by direct or indirect contact.

‧ In 1546, he published a book called On Contagion (《 論 傳染 》).
In this book, he explained the cause of infection, plus treatments
for and precautions against many diseases.

Historical profile (p.21)

Vesalius

Vesalius (1514-1564), a Belgian doctor and anatomist, studied
medicine in France and Italy. He later taught at universities and
became a court physician. Vesalius was one of the first doctors to
perform human dissection. He proved that many previous ideas of
anatomy were wrong. In 1453, he published On the Fabric of the
Human Body. He was regarded as the 'Father of Anatomy’.

Historical profile (p.21)

Copernicus

* Copernicus (1473-1543) was a great scholar. He studied at
universities in Poland and Italy when he was young. He was
an expert on astronomy, medicine and theology. Many people
mistook him for a priest. In 1497, he started to study astronomy.
* Copernicus wrote On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres
(《 天 體 運 行 論 》) and suggested the theory that the Sun was the
centre of the universe. The book was not allowed to circulate
because lt contradicted the Church's explanations about the
universe. It was not published until 1543.

Historical profile (p.21)
Galileo

Galileo (1564-1642) was an Italian astronomer, physicist and
mathematician. In 1609, he made a telescope to observe the
universe. He conducted many physical experiments and proved
that Copernican heliocentrism was correct. However, the Church
did not accept his views and he was put under house arrest by
the Church until his death.

Historical profile (p.26)
Martin Luther

¢ Martin Luther (1483-1546) was born into a worker's family.
His father was a copper miner. He studied philosophy and law
at university, and later he became a priest. In 1517, the Church
started to sell indulgences. He opposed the sale of indulgences
and proposed to reform the Church. He stated that people
could only be saved through faith.

* In 1521, he was excommunicated. He got protection from
Saxony (44) 52 #2), a German state, from the persecution of the
Church. 1525, he married a former nun, with whom he had six
children. In 1534, Luther published a complete translation of
the Bible into German.

Supplementary notes (p.27)

The Anglican Church

* Between 1529 and 1537, Henry VIII of England worked to
separate the English Church from the Catholic Church. He
created an independent Church of England of which he was the
head. This was known as the English Reformation (英格蘭 宗教
改革 ). Henry VIII spread Protestantism throughout the country.

* With the expansion of England and Britain, Anglicanism spread
to places such as North America, Africa and Asia.

Supplementary notes (p.28)
The Thirty Years' War

* In 1618, Ferdinand II (2£#8"4 — {2), the king of Bohemia ( 波
4p 2K S2) and the future Holy Roman emperor, tried to impose
Catholicism on his people. The Protestant nobles of both
Bohemia and Austria revolted against him. The Thirty Years’
War started. Many European countries joined the war because
of religious, dynastic and territorial rivalries.

* The war was one of the longest wars in history. More than
8 million people were killed from battles as well as from
famines and diseases caused by the conflict.

* In 1648, European countries signed the Peace of Westphalia

(《 威 斯 特 伐 利 亞 和 約 》) to end the Thirty Years' War.

Historical profile (p.33)
Kepler

Kepler, a German mathematician and astronomer, was strongly
influenced by the ideas of Copernicus when he was a university
student. He discovered the motions of the planets in the solar

system. They are known as Kepler's Laws (開 普 勒 定律 ).
One of the laws states that the planets move in elliptical
orbits with the Sun at the centre.

Historical profile (p.33)
Newton

Newton was a famous English scientist and mathematician.

He discovered many scientific laws such as the law of universal
gravitation. His theories had a strong impact on science. He 1s
considered by many as the greatest scientist that ever lived. Besides
academic studies, he also took part in politics. He twice entered
Parliament as the representative of the University of Cambridge.

Historical profile (p.37)
Montesquieu

* Montesquieu (1689-1755) suggested that the legislature, the
executive and the judiciary should be separated. His ideas greatly
influenced the government of the United States. The American
government adopts the principle of the separation of powers.

It is divided into the legislature, the executive and the judiciary.
They are kept separated and independent of one another.

* Montesquieu wrote The Spirits of the Laws (法 意 )), which
is a comparative study on republic, monarchy and despotism.
It states that the powers of government should be separated
and balanced to guarantee the freedom of the individual.

Historian's view (p.38)
Significance of the Encyclopaedie

Historian John Hirst comments on the significance of the
Eneyclopaedie in lus book The Shortest History of Europe:

"The Encyclopaedie is notable because it was not, as we think

of encyclopedias today, a staid authority written by established
scholars. This was a radical encyclopedia because it applied reason
to everything and it gave no hierarchy within knowledge. It did not
start, as the church would like, with theology and God. Where do
you find God in this encyclopedia? Under D (for Dieu) and

R (for Religion). This is an alphabetical index to knowledge, and
that very act of making it alphabetical was a defiance of the church
and its claims to possess the highest truths. All knowledge was
treated in the same way and all was subjected to the same test."

Supplementary notes (p.43)

The Enclosure Movement as a movement of 'sheep
devour men’ ( 羊 吃 人 )

Between the late 15th and 16th centuries, the British textile
industry grew rapidly. The price of wool, a raw material of textile,
boomed. In order to raise more sheep for profit, the British nobles
started to enclose farmlands and public land, and build large-scale
pastureland. This is known as the 'Enclosure Movement’. In his
book Utopia, British scholar Thomas More depicted the Enclosure
Movement as a movement of 'sheep devour men’, criticizing its
destruction on the agricultural industry.

Historical profile (p.45)

Stephenson

Stephenson (1781-1848) was born into a poor worker's family. He
only learned to read and write at the age of 18. He worked ln a mine
when he was young. He was very interested in machinery. In 1815,

locomotive was then used on the Liverpool-Manchester Railway.

Smallpox

Smallpox is a highly infectious disease. Once people caught
this disease, they suffered from fever and skin rashes. This
would leave permanent pitted scars on the patients’ skin.
Smallpox was a deadly disease in the past. The World
Health Organization carried out a vaccination campaign

against smallpox in the 1970s. This stopped the spread of the

disease, and smallpox was eliminated in 1979.

Pierre Curie and Marie Curie

Pierre Curie (1859-1906) was a French physicist. He studied physics
at the University of Paris. Mane Sklodowska (1867-1934) was born
in Warsaw, Poland. Curie and Sklodowska married in 1895. In 1903,

Marie Curie won another Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911, but she
was diagnosed with cancer because of her prolonged exposure to
radioactive materials.

Supplementary notes (p.53)

Advantages and disadvantages of the division of labour

* The division of labour was popular during the Industrial
Revolution. It is still widely practiced in the modern industrial and
commercial sectors. This practice has many advantages. Workers
handle only a part of the production process, so worker training is
simplified. Production efficiency can be improved.

* However, it also has many disadvantages. Workers handle
only a part of the production process, making work boring
and leading to low work motivation. Their means to earn a
living are also limited.

Supplementary notes (p.57)

Labour problems

Most government officials and members of parliament came from
wealthy families. They only cared about the interests of the capitalist
class. In the early Industrial Revolution, the working class had

no representatives in parliaments. The rights of the workers were
neglected and exploited, resulting in severe labour problems.

supplementary notes (p.59)
Chartist Movement

In the mid-19th century, European workers started to fight for their
rights. From 1838 to 1848, trade unions in Britain started the Chartist

Movement, demanding the right to vote and be elected. The movement

presented three petitions to Parliament in 1839, 1842 and 1848, but
they were all rejected. The Chartist Movement ended in failure.

Supplementary notes (p.67)

The crisis of the French monarchy

* The extravagance and incapability of Louis XV brought the French
monarchy to a crisis. Internally, France faced frequent farmers and
workers’ strikes. Externally, France was defeated in wars, which

left a huge amount of war debts. The French people's dissatisfaction
towards the government kept increasing, making the Enlightenment

ideas more popular.

* Since 1787, the French people had urged to carry out reforms and
call the Estates-General. Louis XVI decided to call the Estates-
General in 1789, but no reforms were passed during the meeting.
More conflicts between the Third Estate and the other two estates
resulted, bringing France on the brink of revolution.

When the French Revolution broke out, many nobles tried to form an

Supplementary notes (p.77)

Elba

Elba is a small island located to the northeast of Corsica. It has an
area of 223 km’. In 1814, Napoleon was exiled to Elba. During his
ten-month stay on the island, Napoleon ruled the island like a king
and introduced measures to improve the living conditions of the
people there. He built roads, planted olive and white mulberry trees,
and erected street lights. There is a museum about Napoleon on Elba
which shows his life on the island.

Supplementary notes (p.77)
The Battle of Waterloo

In March 1815, Napoleon returned from Elba and ruled France again
for about 100 days. In June, Britain and Prussia attacked France.
The Battle of Waterloo broke out in central Belgium. Napoleon
commanded about 125,000 soldiers. Arthur Wellesley ( 威 斯 利 )
commanded about 93,000 British soldiers and Gebhard Leberecht
von Bliicher (布魯克 ) commanded about 120,000 Prussian soldiers.
The allied forces of Britain and Prussia defeated France. French
casualties were about 32,000, the coalition's about 23,000.

The Holy Alliance was a loose alliance formed in Paris in 1815.
